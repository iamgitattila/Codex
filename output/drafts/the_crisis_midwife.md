# THE CRISIS MIDWIFE
## Delivering Babies, C-Section Emergencies, and Gynecological Crises

**Archetype:** Field Medic
**Emotional Hook:** "Birth is dangerous even WITH hospitals. Without them, it becomes a life-or-death lottery."

---

# CHAPTER 1: CHILDBIRTH WITHOUT HOSPITALS
## The Most Dangerous Day in Human Life

Childbirth is the most common medical emergency you're unprepared for.

Here's the reality: In developed nations with hospital access, maternal mortality is about 17 deaths per 100,000 births. That's 0.017%—remarkably safe.

Without medical care? Historical rates show 1-2% maternal mortality. That's 1,000-2,000 deaths per 100,000 births.

Modern medicine didn't make childbirth safe through magic. It made it safe through specific interventions for specific complications. Remove those interventions, and those complications become fatal.

This chapter teaches you what those interventions are and how to perform them when hospitals aren't available.

**Why Childbirth Is So Dangerous**

Human childbirth is uniquely difficult among mammals for evolutionary reasons:

First, we walk upright. Bipedalism narrowed the birth canal. Our pelvises adapted for walking, not birthing.

Second, we have enormous heads. Human brain size expanded faster than pelvis size could compensate. The result: tight fit, difficult passage.

Third, we're born premature compared to other primates. A human infant is helpless for months. This is evolutionary compromise—any later, and the head wouldn't fit through the birth canal at all.

These three factors make human birth inherently risky.

Most births proceed normally despite this. The human body evolved sophisticated mechanisms to manage the process. But when complications occur—and they will in 10-20% of births—intervention becomes critical.

The most common lethal complications:

**Hemorrhage:** Excessive bleeding after delivery. The leading cause of maternal death globally. Without oxytocin or surgical intervention, it kills quickly.

**Obstructed labor:** Baby can't fit through birth canal despite hours of trying. Mother exhausts herself. Baby suffocates. Both may die without C-section.

**Eclampsia:** Pregnancy-induced seizures. Can kill mother and baby. Requires magnesium sulfate or emergency delivery.

**Infection:** Childbed fever killed millions historically. Bacteria enter during delivery. Without antibiotics, spreads to sepsis.

**Cord prolapse:** Umbilical cord comes out before baby. Cord gets compressed. Baby loses oxygen. Emergency C-section required.

**Placental abruption:** Placenta detaches early. Mother hemorrhages internally. Baby suffocates. Emergency delivery required immediately.

**Shoulder dystocia:** Baby's head delivers but shoulders stuck. Baby suffocates if not freed within minutes. Requires specific maneuvers.

Each of these complications requires specific knowledge to manage. Hospital staff train for years to handle them. You're going to learn the essential interventions in this book.

**What You Need to Know vs. What You'll Actually Do**

Let me be direct about something: Much of modern obstetrics requires tools, medications, and skills you won't have in a collapse scenario.

I can't teach you how to perform a C-section in a chapter. That requires surgical training, sterile environment, anesthesia, and post-operative care beyond field medicine capability.

But I can teach you:

- How to recognize when C-section is necessary
- How to manage most deliveries safely without medical intervention
- How to handle the most common complications using basic tools
- How to know when you're in over your head
- How to make terrible choices when both options are bad

This book operates on realistic assumptions:

You have basic medical supplies (covered in Chapter 2). You have clean water. You have improvised tools. You do NOT have an operating room, anesthesiologist, or blood bank.

Under these constraints, your role is to:

1. Support normal delivery (85-90% of cases)
2. Manage common complications (another 5-8%)
3. Recognize when you're facing unsurvivable complications (remaining 2-5%)

That third category is the hardest part of crisis medicine. Sometimes, despite your best efforts, people die. Knowing when to accept this versus when to attempt desperate measures is crucial.

**The Stages of Labor**

Before we discuss complications, understand normal labor:

**Stage One: Cervical Dilation (8-20 hours for first baby, 4-8 for subsequent)**

Uterus contracts regularly, dilating cervix from 0 to 10 centimeters. This is the longest stage.

Early labor (0-6cm): Contractions 15-20 minutes apart, 30-60 seconds long. Mother can usually talk through them.

Active labor (6-8cm): Contractions 3-5 minutes apart, 45-60 seconds long. Mother focused, serious, sometimes scared.

Transition (8-10cm): Contractions 2-3 minutes apart, 60-90 seconds long. Most painful phase. Mother may panic, say she can't do it, want drugs. This is normal.

Your role: Support, hydration, positioning, encouragement. Don't intervene unless problems arise.

**Stage Two: Pushing and Delivery (30 minutes to 3 hours)**

Cervix fully dilated. Mother pushes with contractions. Baby descends birth canal. Head crowns (visible at opening). Head delivers. Shoulders rotate and deliver. Body slips out.

Your role: Support mother's chosen position. Guide but don't rush. Catch baby. Ensure baby breathes.

**Stage Three: Placenta Delivery (5-30 minutes)**

Uterus contracts further, shearing placenta from wall. Placenta delivers. Uterus clamps down on blood vessels.

Your role: Gentle cord traction if needed. Ensure complete placenta delivery. Massage uterus to prevent hemorrhage.

**Stage Four: Immediate Postpartum (First 2 hours)**

Highest risk period for hemorrhage. Monitor mother's bleeding, vital signs, uterine tone.

Your role: Constant vigilance. This is when mothers die if you're not watching.

Understand these stages intimately. Most complications announce themselves by deviations from this normal pattern.

**Setting Up Your Birth Space**

Preparation prevents complications.

Location requirements:

**Warmth:** Hypothermia kills newborns. Birth space must be warm (75-80°F minimum).

**Light:** You need to see what you're doing. Natural light is best. Have backup lighting.

**Privacy:** Mother needs to feel safe. This isn't modesty—it's neurology. Fear delays labor.

**Cleanliness:** Reduce infection risk. Clean surfaces, clean hands, clean supplies.

**Water access:** Hydration for mother. Cleaning for baby and mother. Sterilizing tools.

Essential supplies (detailed in Chapter 2):

- Clean towels and blankets (lots)
- Clean, sharp scissors or knife for cord cutting
- String or clamps for cord tying
- Bulb syringe for clearing baby's airway
- Large bowl for placenta
- Plastic sheet or tarp under mother
- Clean clothes for mother and baby
- Water for drinking and cleaning

Optional but valuable:

- Blood pressure cuff
- Stethoscope
- Flashlight
- Heating source for baby
- Honey or sugar (for newborn hypoglycemia)
- Herbs for hemorrhage control (covered in Chapter 5)

**Positioning for Birth**

Hospital births typically use flat-on-back position. This is for doctor convenience, not physiological benefit.

Better positions for unassisted birth:

**Squatting:** Uses gravity. Opens pelvis 10-15% wider. Faster delivery. This is how most mammals birth.

**Hands and knees:** Good for back labor. Helps baby rotate. Reduces perineal tearing.

**Side-lying:** Good for rest between pushes. Reduces pressure on major blood vessels.

**Semi-sitting:** Supported by partner or wall. Compromise between lying and squatting.

**Standing/swaying:** Early labor. Movement helps baby descend.

Let mother choose and change positions freely. Her body knows what it needs. Your job is support, not direction.

Exception: If complications develop requiring intervention, you may need her in specific position for access.

**The Critical First Hour**

Most immediate postpartum deaths occur within the first hour. Here's your checklist:

Immediate (first 60 seconds):

- Baby breathing? (Should cry or breathe within 30 seconds)
- Baby pink? (Hands and feet may be blue briefly—that's okay)
- Cord still pulsing? (Don't cut until pulsing stops, typically 2-5 minutes)

First 5 minutes:

- Dry and warm baby immediately (prevents hypothermia)
- Place baby on mother's chest (skin-to-skin)
- Cover both with blanket
- Baby should root and attempt nursing

First 30 minutes:

- Placenta should deliver (don't pull; gentle traction only)
- Check placenta for completeness (must get all of it)
- Massage uterine fundus (prevents hemorrhage)
- Monitor mother for excessive bleeding

First hour:

- Baby should nurse successfully (stimulates uterine contraction)
- Mother's bleeding should slow to heavy-period level
- Both should be alert and responsive
- No signs of hemorrhage or infection

This first hour determines survival. Everything else—cord cutting, weighing baby, bathing—is secondary.

Stay vigilant. Most emergencies announce themselves in this window.

# CHAPTER 2: MANAGING NORMAL DELIVERY
## The 85% of Births That Proceed Without Complications

Most births, even without medical intervention, proceed normally.

Your job is to support the process, not control it. Birth is not a medical procedure requiring management. It's a physiological process requiring support.

The interventions that dramatically reduced maternal mortality were for complications, not normal birth. For uncomplicated labor, the best intervention is often minimal intervention.

This chapter teaches you how to support normal birth and recognize when "normal" has shifted to "complicated."

**Early Labor: The Waiting Game**

Early labor can last 12-24 hours for first-time mothers. This is normal. It's also tedious.

Your role during early labor:

**Time contractions:** Count frequency (minutes from start of one to start of next) and duration (how long each lasts). This tells you progression.

**Encourage movement:** Walking, swaying, position changes help baby descend and labor progress.

**Ensure hydration and calories:** Labor is athletic event. Mother needs fuel. Water, juice, simple carbs.

**Provide emotional support:** Fear and anxiety can slow labor. Keep environment calm.

**Allow rest between contractions:** This isn't time to be coaching. Let mother conserve energy.

When to start worrying during early labor:

- Contractions for 24+ hours with no progression
- Sudden severe pain (not contraction pattern)
- Heavy bleeding before active labor
- Loss of consciousness or confusion
- High fever (>100.4°F)
- Green or brown amniotic fluid (fetal distress)

If any of these occur, reference emergency protocols (Chapter 3).

**Active Labor: Focus and Support**

Once contractions are 3-5 minutes apart, lasting 45-60 seconds, active labor has begun.

Mother will be more focused now. Less talking. More serious. This is when she needs your presence more than your advice.

Effective support techniques:

**Counter-pressure:** Press firmly on lower back during contractions. This relieves back labor pain.

**Hip squeeze:** Stand behind mother. Place hands on both hip bones. Squeeze inward during contractions. This opens pelvis and relieves pressure.

**Breathing guidance:** Slow, deep breaths during contractions. Don't hyperventilate. "Breathe with me" is often enough.

**Cool cloths:** For face and neck. Labor is hot work.

**Verbal encouragement:** "You're doing great." "Baby's coming." "This contraction is working." Simple affirmations matter.

**Physical support:** If squatting, support her weight. If standing, let her lean on you.

What NOT to do:

- Don't tell her to calm down (physiologically impossible)
- Don't argue about positions (her body knows better than you)
- Don't give running commentary (she needs to focus inward)
- Don't rush her (birth has its own timeline)
- Don't check cervical dilation repeatedly (painful, unnecessary, infection risk)

Your primary job: Be present, be supportive, and don't make things worse.

**Transition: The Hardest Part**

Transition phase—8cm to 10cm dilation—is usually shortest but most intense.

Mother may:

- Say she can't do it anymore
- Want to give up
- Demand pain medication that doesn't exist
- Get angry with you
- Feel nauseous or vomit
- Feel uncontrollable urge to push

All of this is NORMAL. It means birth is close.

Your response: "You're in transition. This means you're almost done. Baby's coming soon."

This reassurance—that the worst part means the end is near—helps enormously.

DO NOT let her push until you see baby's head crowning. Pushing before full dilation can cause cervical swelling and obstruct labor.

How to know she's fully dilated without checking:

- Uncontrollable urge to push
- Bearing down involuntarily
- Pressure in rectum (feels like needing to poop)
- Change in vocalization (lower, grunting sounds)
- Purple line on buttocks crack (really—this rises with dilation)

When these signs appear, she can push.

**Pushing and Crowning**

Once fully dilated, contractions often space out (body's rest period). Then pushing urge returns.

Let mother push according to her body. Don't count. Don't direct. Let instinct guide.

Effective pushing: Mother pulls knees back, takes deep breath, pushes while holding breath, releases. Repeats 2-3 times per contraction.

You'll see baby's head appear at vaginal opening, then retreat as contraction ends. This is normal. Gradually, head stays visible (crowning).

**CRITICAL MOMENT:** When head crowns, place your hand gently against perineum (tissue between vagina and anus). Apply gentle counter-pressure as baby emerges. This helps prevent tearing.

Tell mother: "Stop pushing. Pant. Let your body ease baby out."

Head emerges slowly. As it delivers, support it with your hand. Don't pull.

After head is out, check for cord around neck (nuchal cord):

- Feel for cord with finger
- If present and loose, slip it over baby's head
- If tight, you may need to cut it now (covered in Chapter 3)
- If no cord, proceed

**Shoulder Delivery**

This is where complications can occur (shoulder dystocia—Chapter 3). In normal birth:

Baby's head will rotate on its own (facing mother's thigh rather than down).

Next contraction, guide head gently downward (toward mother's back). Front shoulder delivers.

Then guide gently upward. Back shoulder delivers.

Body slips out easily.

**CATCH THE BABY.** They're slippery. Use both hands. Support head and body.

**Immediate Newborn Care**

First 60 seconds after birth:

Wipe fluid from face with clean cloth. Clear mouth and nose gently with finger or bulb syringe if needed.

Rub baby's back firmly. This stimulates breathing.

Baby should cry or breathe within 30 seconds. If not—refer to Chapter 3 (emergency protocols).

Once breathing, place baby on mother's bare chest immediately. Cover both with blanket.

Leave cord intact until it stops pulsing (2-5 minutes). This transfers remaining blood to baby.

**Placenta Delivery**

10-30 minutes after baby, mother will feel mild contractions. Placenta is separating.

Signs placenta has detached:

- Gush of blood
- Cord lengthens
- Uterus firms up and rises in abdomen

When these occur, support cord gently. Ask mother to push gently. Placenta should slide out.

DO NOT pull on cord forcefully. This can cause hemorrhage or retained placenta (medical emergency).

Once placenta delivers, inspect it:

Must be complete. Looks like liver. Should be intact with membranes attached.

If pieces missing, mother needs medical evacuation (retained placenta causes hemorrhage and infection).

After placenta: Massage uterine fundus (top of uterus, at belly button level). Should feel firm like grapefruit. Massage keeps it contracted, preventing hemorrhage.

**The First Hour Postpartum**

Both mother and baby are vulnerable. Stay vigilant.

Monitor mother:

- Bleeding: Should slow to heavy period level. Soaking more than one pad per hour is excessive (Chapter 3).
- Uterus: Should stay firm. If soft and boggy, massage immediately.
- Vital signs: Should be stable. Elevated heart rate or low blood pressure indicate hemorrhage.

Monitor baby:

- Breathing: Should be regular, not labored
- Color: Should be pink (hands/feet may be bluish briefly)
- Temperature: Keep warm. Hypothermia is major newborn risk.
- Nursing: Should root and attempt to latch within first hour

If both mother and baby are stable after first hour, immediate danger has passed.

But stay alert for 24 hours. Late hemorrhage can occur. Newborn complications can develop.

Your job isn't finished at delivery. It's finished when both are stable and thriving.

# CHAPTER 3: EMERGENCY COMPLICATIONS
## When Everything Goes Wrong

This chapter covers the complications that kill mothers and babies.

Some are manageable with field medicine. Some require equipment you won't have. Some require choosing who lives.

I won't sugar-coat this: You will face scenarios where both mother and baby can't be saved. Where your intervention determines who dies.

This is the hardest part of crisis medicine. Not the technical skills—the moral weight.

**Hemorrhage: The Silent Killer**

Postpartum hemorrhage is the leading cause of maternal death worldwide. Blood loss exceeding 500ml after delivery is considered hemorrhage.

Signs of postpartum hemorrhage:

- Soaking through more than one pad per hour
- Large clots (bigger than golf ball)
- Dizziness, weakness, confusion
- Pale, cold, clammy skin
- Rapid pulse, low blood pressure
- Continued bleeding despite massage

Primary causes:

**Uterine atony:** Uterus fails to contract after delivery. Accounts for 80% of hemorrhages.

Treatment:

1. Bimanual uterine massage: One hand on abdomen massaging uterus. Other hand in vagina pushing uterus up. This is aggressive and uncomfortable but life-saving.

2. Nipple stimulation/nursing: Releases oxytocin naturally. Have baby nurse or stimulate nipples manually.

3. Herbal oxytocics: Shepherd's purse, angelica root, motherwort tincture. 10-30 drops under tongue.

4. Pitocin if available: 10 units IM. This is prescription but save lives. Obtain if possible pre-crisis.

5. Methergine if available: 0.2mg IM. Another pharmaceutical option.

6. If bleeding doesn't stop: Pack uterus with clean gauze soaked in antiseptic. This is desperate measure. Buy time for evacuation.

**Retained placenta:** Placenta doesn't deliver or pieces remain.

Treatment:

1. Wait up to one hour if mother stable.

2. Controlled cord traction: Steady, gentle pulling while counter-pressure on uterus.

3. Manual removal: This is last resort. Reach into uterus, feel for placenta, gently separate from wall. Risk of infection and perforation is high.

4. If you can't remove it: She needs surgical intervention. Attempt evacuation.

**Lacerations:** Tears in cervix, vagina, or perineum bleeding excessively.

Treatment:

1. Direct pressure with clean gauze.

2. If that fails: Suture the bleeding vessel. Use curved needle, absorbable suture. This requires practice.

3. Ice packs reduce swelling and slow bleeding.

Without surgical intervention, severe lacerations can bleed out. Apply pressure, attempt repair, pray.

**Hemorrhage Triage:**

If bleeding continues despite all interventions, you're facing death. If blood products and surgery available, she might survive. Without them, she won't.

Your decision: Stay with her and provide comfort, or save baby by leaving with it. There's no good answer.

**Shoulder Dystocia: The Nightmare Delivery**

Head delivers. Shoulders stuck. Baby's oxygen supply cut off. You have 4-5 minutes before brain damage. 7-8 minutes before death.

Signs:

- Head delivers but retracts ("turtle sign")
- Head appears but shoulders don't deliver with normal effort
- Body won't deliver despite pushing

Treatment (McRoberts Maneuver + Suprapubic Pressure):

1. Immediate: Remove mother's pants/clothing. Get knee access.

2. McRoberts: Grab mother's legs. Flex knees to chest. Press knees down and outward. This rotates pelvis, often freeing shoulder.

3. Suprapubic pressure: Have partner press HARD on pubic bone (not fundus) from behind front shoulder. This pushes shoulder under pubic bone.

4. If that fails: Perform Rubin maneuver: Reach into vagina. Find front shoulder. Push it toward baby's chest. This narrows shoulder width.

5. If that fails: Perform Wood's screw: Reach behind posterior shoulder. Push it forward. This rotates baby.

6. If that fails: Remove posterior arm: Reach in, grab baby's posterior arm, sweep across chest, deliver arm first.

7. If that fails: Zavanelli maneuver: Push baby's head back up, perform emergency C-section. Without surgical capability, baby dies here.

Practice these maneuvers on anatomical models if possible. In crisis, you won't have time to read instructions.

Shoulder dystocia is survivable with quick, aggressive intervention. Hesitation kills.

**Cord Prolapse: The Race Against Time**

Umbilical cord comes out before baby. Cord gets compressed between baby and pelvis. Baby loses oxygen.

Signs:

- See or feel cord in vagina
- Sudden fetal distress
- Cord visible at vaginal opening

Treatment:

1. KEEP CORD WET. Dry cord = dead baby.

2. Push cord back into vagina gently. Keep moist.

3. Mother on hands and knees, butt elevated. Gravity takes pressure off cord.

4. Reach into vagina. Push baby's presenting part UP, away from cord. Maintain pressure.

5. THIS IS HOLDING POSITION ONLY. Baby must deliver within 30 minutes or brain damage occurs.

6. If delivery not imminent, this is emergency C-section indication. Without surgical capability, baby dies.

Cord prolapse has 50-60% survival rate with immediate intervention. Without C-section capability, it's usually fatal.

**Breech Birth: When Baby Comes Wrong**

Head supposed to come first. In breech, butt or feet come first.

Types:

- Frank breech: Butt first, legs extended
- Complete breech: Butt first, legs crossed
- Footling breech: Feet first

Breech delivery is possible but dangerous. Risk of head entrapment—body delivers but head stuck.

If you diagnose breech before labor, attempt external version: Push on abdomen, rotate baby head-down. Success rate 50-60%. If successful, proceed with normal delivery.

If breech birth is only option:

Let body deliver spontaneously. Do NOT pull on baby.

Body will deliver to umbilicus. Loop of cord should deliver too.

Let baby "hang" from birth canal. Gravity helps head position.

When back of neck appears, lift baby's body upward toward mother's abdomen. This flexes head.

Head should deliver. If doesn't deliver within 3 minutes, baby asphyxiates.

Last resort if head stuck: Mauriceau-Smellie-Veit maneuver: Support baby's body on your forearm. Insert fingers of that hand into baby's mouth. Pull gently downward while pressing above pubic bone. This may deliver head.

Breech delivery has high mortality without surgical backup. Only attempt if no other option.
