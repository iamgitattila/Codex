## Growing, Harvesting, and Processing Wheat, Corn, and Oats Without Industrial Equipment

# CHAPTER 1: WHY GRAIN IS THE FOUNDATION OF CIVILIZATION
## The Crop That Changes Everything

Every civilization in human history was built on grain.

Mesopotamia: Wheat and barley.
Egypt: Wheat.
China: Rice and millet.
Mesoamerica: Corn.
Europe: Wheat, rye, oats, barley.

Grain is what allows humans to move beyond subsistence hunting and gathering. Grain can be stored for years. Grain can feed armies, cities, and empires. Grain makes civilization possible.

Yet modern homesteaders ignore it.

They grow tomatoes, lettuce, peppers, beans. These are important. But they're not calorie crops. You cannot live on vegetables alone.

**Humans need approximately 2,000 calories per day.** A family of four needs roughly 2.9 million calories per year.

Let's do the math on common homestead crops:

**Tomatoes:** 18 calories per 100 grams. You'd need to grow and eat 320 pounds of tomatoes per person per day to meet caloric needs. Impossible.

**Lettuce:** 15 calories per 100 grams. Even worse.

**Potatoes:** 77 calories per 100 grams. Better, but you'd need 6 pounds per person per day. That's 2,200 pounds per person per year. Possible, but requires significant storage and effort.

**Wheat:** 340 calories per 100 grams. You need 1.35 pounds per person per day. That's 490 pounds per person per year. Doable.

**Corn:** 365 calories per 100 grams. You need 1.25 pounds per person per day. That's 456 pounds per person per year.

See the difference?

Grain provides 4-5x the calories per pound compared to potatoes, and 20x compared to vegetables. Grain is dense nutrition. Grain stores for years without refrigeration. Grain can be ground into flour for bread, cooked as porridge, fermented into beer, or fed to livestock.

**Grain is not optional for serious food security. It's foundational.**

**The Acre That Feeds You**

One acre of wheat, properly managed, yields 40-60 bushels. One bushel of wheat weighs 60 pounds. That's 2,400-3,600 pounds of wheat per acre.

A family of four needs about 2,000 pounds of wheat per year for complete caloric sufficiency (assuming you're also eating meat, dairy, vegetables for micronutrients and variety).

**One acre of wheat feeds your family for a year. With surplus.**

Compare this to vegetable gardening: Even an excellent vegetable garden of 1 acre produces maybe 10,000-20,000 pounds of vegetables (mostly water weight). But those vegetables provide perhaps 200,000-400,000 calories total. That feeds one person for 100-200 days, not a year.

One acre of wheat produces 3 million+ calories. That feeds a family of four for an entire year.

This is why grain built civilization. Nothing else comes close to the calorie density per acre.

**Why Modern Homesteaders Don't Grow Grain**

Four reasons:

**1. Perceived difficulty:** People assume grain farming requires massive equipment. Combines, tractors, grain dryers, silos. It doesn't. Humans grew grain for 10,000 years before mechanization. You can too.

**2. Processing challenge:** Wheat must be threshed (removed from stalks), winnowed (chaff separated from grain), and ground (flour). These steps seem complicated. They're not. They're just unfamiliar.

**3. Garden-focused culture:** Homestead media emphasizes gardens. Books, blogs, YouTube channels—all focused on tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers. Grain is ignored because it's not photogenic and doesn't fit the hobby-garden aesthetic.

**4. Small-scale mindset:** People think "I'll just buy flour." This works until stores are empty, supply chains collapse, or currency becomes worthless. Then flour isn't available at any price. Those who can't grow their own starve.

**The Grains That Matter**

Dozens of grain crops exist. Only a few matter for homestead food security:

**Wheat (Triticum spp.):**

The foundational grain of Western civilization. Two main types:

**Winter wheat:** Planted in fall (September-October), overwinters as small plant, matures in early summer (June-July). Higher yields than spring wheat. Preferred for most climates. Varieties: Hard red winter (bread flour), soft white winter (pastry flour).

**Spring wheat:** Planted in early spring (March-April), matures in late summer (August-September). Lower yields but necessary in climates with harsh winters that kill winter wheat. Varieties: Hard red spring (bread flour), durum (pasta).

**Typical yield:** 40-60 bushels per acre (2,400-3,600 lbs) with good management.

**Best for:** Bread, flour tortillas, pasta, pastries, thickening agent, livestock feed.

**Corn (Zea mays):**

The foundational grain of Americas. Highest calorie yield per acre of any grain. Three types:

**Dent corn:** "Field corn." Dried and ground into cornmeal, grits, polenta. Fed to livestock. Stores for years. This is the corn for homesteads, not sweet corn (which is vegetable, not grain).

**Flint corn:** Hard kernels, excellent for long-term storage. Traditional Native American varieties. Beautiful colored kernels. Ground for cornmeal or nixtamalized for hominy/masa.

**Flour corn:** Soft starch, easy to grind. Lower yield than dent or flint. Historic varieties still grown by traditional communities.

**Typical yield:** 100-150 bushels per acre (5,600-8,400 lbs) with good management. Higher than wheat.

**Best for:** Cornbread, tortillas, polenta, grits, hominy, livestock feed (excellent for pigs and poultry).

**Oats (Avena sativa):**

Incredibly hardy grain. Tolerates poor soil and cool, wet conditions where wheat struggles. Lower yield than wheat or corn but reliable.

**Typical yield:** 60-80 bushels per acre (1,920-2,560 lbs).

**Best for:** Oatmeal, porridge, granola, livestock feed (especially horses), green manure (plowed under for soil improvement).

**Rye (Secale cereale):**

Hardiest grain. Grows in poorest soils. Tolerates cold, drought, and disease better than wheat. Used historically when wheat failed. Strong flavor (acquired taste for some).

**Typical yield:** 30-50 bushels per acre (1,680-2,800 lbs).

**Best for:** Rye bread, pumpernickel, whiskey, livestock feed, cover crop (excellent for soil building).

**Barley (Hordeum vulgare):**

Ancient grain. Drought-tolerant. Faster maturity than wheat (useful for short growing seasons). Primarily livestock feed and brewing (malted barley for beer).

**Typical yield:** 50-70 bushels per acre (2,400-3,360 lbs).

**Best for:** Livestock feed, brewing, barley soup, flour (mixed with wheat flour for bread).

**For most homesteaders, focus on wheat and corn.** These provide the best combination of yield, nutrition, versatility, and storability.

Add oats if you have horses or cool, wet climate. Add rye if you have poor soil or harsh winters. Add barley if you're brewing or need fast-maturing grain.

**Land Requirements for Grain Self-Sufficiency**

Family of four, complete grain independence:

**Wheat:** 1-2 acres (provides flour for bread, baking, pasta)
**Corn:** 0.5-1 acre (provides cornmeal, grits, polenta)
**Oats:** 0.5 acre (provides oatmeal, horse feed if applicable)

**Total: 2-3.5 acres of grain production provides complete carbohydrate self-sufficiency for family of four, with surplus for livestock feed and trade.**

This is achievable on most homestead properties.

Compare to industrial system: Average American consumes 197 pounds of wheat flour and 160 pounds of corn products annually. Multiply by family of four: 788 pounds wheat, 640 pounds corn. You'll produce 2-3x this amount on 2-3 acres, providing massive surplus for security and trade.

**The Tools You Actually Need**

Grain farming without industrial equipment requires tools. But not expensive tools. Most can be purchased used or fabricated.

**For planting:**

**Broadcast seeder:** Hand-crank device worn on chest. Spreads seed evenly while walking field. $30-$80. Can also broadcast by hand (ancient method, less even coverage).

**Seed drill (optional):** Push or horse-drawn device that plants seed in rows at consistent depth. Increases yield by 20-40% compared to broadcasting (less wasted seed, better spacing). Antique models available $200-$800. Modern small-scale models $400-$1,500.

**For harvesting:**

**Scythe:** Long curved blade on wooden handle. Used to cut grain stalks. Takes skill but incredibly efficient once learned. $150-$300 for quality scythe. Will last lifetime with proper maintenance.

**Sickle:** Short curved blade, handheld. Slower than scythe but easier to learn. Backup tool. $20-$50.

**Cradle scythe (optional):** Scythe with attached wooden frame that catches cut grain and lays it in neat rows. Speeds harvesting significantly. $250-$400.

**For threshing:**

**Flail:** Wooden handle with free-swinging beater. Strike grain to separate seeds from stalks. Ancient tool, still effective. $40-$100 or DIY.

**Threshing floor:** Clean, hard surface (concrete, wood, packed earth, tarp). Lay grain stalks, beat with flail or allow animals to trample. Free if using existing surface.

**For winnowing:**

**Basket and wind:** Traditional method. Toss threshed grain in air. Wind blows away chaff, grain falls back into basket. Free, requires breezy day.

**Window fan:** Modern shortcut. Blow fan across falling grain. Same effect as wind method but controlled. $30-$50 for fan.

**For grinding:**

**Hand-crank grain mill:** Essential tool. Adjustable coarseness. Grinds wheat, corn, oats into flour or meal. $50 (basic) to $500 (premium). Buy quality—you'll use it weekly for years.

**Stone mill (traditional):** Two large circular stones, one stationary (bed stone) and one rotating (runner stone). Grain fed between stones, crushed into flour. Can be hand-powered, animal-powered, or water-powered. Historic method. $300-$3,000 depending on size and source.

**Electric grain mill (if grid available):** Fast and efficient. $150-$400. But reliance on electricity is vulnerability.

**Total tool investment for small-scale grain production: $500-$2,000.** Compare to combine harvester: $300,000-$500,000. Your investment is infinitesimal and human-powered.

# CHAPTER 2: GROWING GRAIN FROM SOIL PREPARATION TO HARVEST
## Planting, Managing, and Harvesting Wheat and Corn

**Field Preparation: Getting Soil Ready**

Grain needs prepared soil. Not as meticulously prepared as garden vegetables, but you can't just scatter seed on grass and expect results.

**Breaking new ground:**

If you're converting pasture or unused land to grain production, soil must be broken up.

**Method 1 - Plowing:** Turn soil over with plow (tractor-drawn, horse-drawn, or walking tractor). Buries grass and weeds, exposes fresh soil. Best done in fall for spring planting (allows organic matter to decompose over winter).

**Method 2 - Tilling:** Rototiller breaks up soil mechanically. Faster than hand work, less thorough than plowing. Acceptable for small plots (under 1 acre).

**Method 3 - Solarizing:** Cover area with clear plastic for 6-8 weeks during summer heat. Kills weeds and grass. Remove plastic, till or rake soil, plant. Slow but requires no heavy equipment.

**Method 4 - Pigs:** Fence area, put pigs on it for 2-3 months. They'll root up everything, fertilize soil, and leave it ready for planting. Requires pig fence and pigs, but this is heritage agricultural method that works beautifully.

**Soil fertility:**

Grain depletes soil. Each harvest removes nutrients. You must replace them or yields will decline.

**Organic matter:** Add compost, aged manure, or cover crops plowed under (green manure). Maintains soil structure and provides slow-release nutrients.

**Nitrogen:** Most critical nutrient for grain. Sources: Manure (best), compost, legume cover crops (plow under peas, clover, or vetch before grain planting—they fix atmospheric nitrogen in soil).

**Phosphorus and Potassium:** Rock phosphate and greensand (slow-release natural fertilizers). Wood ash (excellent potassium source). Bone meal (phosphorus).

**Crop rotation:** Never grow grain in same field consecutive years. Rotate: Year 1 grain, Year 2 legume cover crop or pasture, Year 3 grain again. This prevents disease buildup, restores nitrogen, and maintains yields.

**Planting Wheat**

**Timing:**

**Winter wheat:** Plant September-October, depending on region. Goal: Plant early enough that wheat germinates and grows 3-5 inches before winter, but not so early that it grows too large (tall wheat is more vulnerable to winter kill). 6 weeks before first hard freeze is typical target.

**Spring wheat:** Plant as soon as soil can be worked in spring (March-April). Earlier is better—wheat needs cool temperatures during grain formation.

**Seeding rate:** 90-120 pounds of seed per acre for winter wheat, 100-130 pounds for spring wheat (slightly higher rate compensates for lower tillering).

**Method:**

**Broadcasting:** Walk field in overlapping passes with broadcast seeder. Spread seed evenly. After broadcasting, lightly rake or drag to cover seed with 1-2 inches of soil. Or allow rainfall to work seed into soil (less reliable).

**Drilling:** Use seed drill to plant in rows, 6-8 inches apart, 1-2 inches deep. More efficient use of seed, higher yields. Worth the investment if growing more than 1 acre.

**Managing wheat:**

Wheat is low-maintenance crop. Once planted, it mostly takes care of itself.

**Weed control:** Critical first 4-6 weeks. Hand-pull or hoe weeds competing with young wheat. Once wheat is 6+ inches tall, it shades out most weeds.

**Pest control:** Rare in small-scale production. Grasshoppers can damage wheat in dry years. Birds eat some grain at harvest (accept as loss or use bird netting). Rust, smut, and fungal diseases occur but are less common in diverse small-scale settings than monoculture industrial fields.

**Irrigation:** Usually unnecessary if planting is timed correctly (fall for winter wheat, early spring for spring wheat). Wheat is drought-tolerant once established. Only irrigate if severe drought during grain-filling stage (late May-June).

**Harvesting wheat:**

Wheat is ready when:
- Stalks turn golden brown
- Grain is hard (can't dent with fingernail)
- Moisture content is 13-15% (grain breaks rather than mashes)
- Heads droop downward

**Timing:** Late June to early July for winter wheat, late August to early September for spring wheat (regional variation).

**Method:**

**Scything:** Grasp bunch of wheat stalks with one hand, slice through at base with scythe, lay cut stalks on ground in neat row. Work your way across field. With practice, one person can harvest 0.25-0.5 acre per day.

**Bundling:** After cutting, gather stalks into bundles (sheaves). Tie with twine or twist of straw. Stand bundles upright in shocks (teepee formation, 6-10 bundles per shock). This allows further drying in field and protects grain from rain.

**Field drying:** Leave shocks in field 1-2 weeks for complete drying (critical to prevent mold during storage).

**Bringing in harvest:** Carry or cart dried sheaves to barn or covered area for threshing.

**Planting Corn**

**Timing:** Plant after last frost when soil temperature reaches 60°F (typically late April to May). Corn needs warm soil to germinate.

**Seeding rate:** 24,000-30,000 seeds per acre for dent/flint corn. That's about 8-10 pounds of seed per acre.

**Method:**

Corn must be planted in rows (unlike wheat which can be broadcast). Corn benefits from cross-pollination and structured spacing.

**Row spacing:** 30-36 inches between rows, 8-12 inches between plants within row.

**Planting technique:**

**Hand-planting:** Use hoe to make furrow or use dibber (pointed stick) to make holes. Drop 2-3 seeds per spot, cover with 1-2 inches of soil. Thin to strongest plant once sprouted. Labor-intensive but works for small plots.

**Push seeder:** Mechanical push-style corn planter. Adjustable spacing, plants one seed at a time at correct depth. Dramatically faster than hand-planting. Worth investment for plots over 0.25 acre. $100-$400.

**Horse-drawn planter:** Antique corn planters still available. Pulled by horse or riding tractor. Plants multiple rows simultaneously. Essential for acreage over 1-2 acres. $300-$1,500 for used equipment.

**Managing corn:**

**Weed control:** Essential first 4-6 weeks. Cultivate between rows with hoe, wheel hoe, or horse-drawn cultivator. Once corn is knee-high, it shades weeds and further cultivation is less necessary (but still beneficial).

**Hilling:** When corn is 6-8 inches tall, pull soil up around base of stalks. This supports plants against wind, encourages root development, and smothers weeds.

**Pest control:**

**Corn borers:** Caterpillars that tunnel into stalks and ears. Hand-pick if visible. BT spray (organic bacterial insecticide) works if infestation is severe.

**Raccoons:** MAJOR pest for corn. They love corn and will destroy crop overnight. Solutions: Electric fence around corn plot (two strands, 6 and 12 inches above ground). Dogs patrolling property. Harvest promptly when mature.

**Deer:** Also love corn. Tall fence (7+ feet) or electric fence. Dogs help.

**Birds:** Crows and blackbirds eat seeds and young sprouts. Less problematic for mature corn.

**Irrigation:** Corn needs consistent moisture, especially during tasseling and grain-fill (July-August). If rainfall is insufficient (less than 1 inch per week), irrigate. Drought during critical growth period significantly reduces yield.

**Harvesting corn:**

Corn is ready when:
- Husks turn brown and dry
- Kernels are hard and dent when pressed (thus "dent corn")
- Moisture content is below 20%

**Timing:** September-October, depending on variety and planting date. Field corn is harvested much later than sweet corn (which is picked at milk stage).

**Method:**

**Hand-picking:** Walk rows, snap ears from stalks. Drop ears in bucket or toss into wagon/cart. One person can harvest 0.1-0.2 acre per day.

**Corn crib drying:** Store ears in corn crib (ventilated structure that allows air flow around corn). Corn continues drying in crib for 4-8 weeks until moisture drops to safe storage level (13-15%). Then shell (remove kernels from cob).

**Field drying:** Alternative method—leave corn on stalks in field until husks are completely dry and papery. Then harvest. Risks: bird damage, rodent damage, weather damage, raccoons. Advantage: No need for corn crib.

**Shelling corn:**

Once dried, kernels must be removed from cob.

**Hand-shelling:** Grasp ear, twist and rub kernels off cob with thumb and fingers. Slow, hard on hands. Works for small amounts.

**Hand-crank corn sheller:** Mechanical device that removes kernels from cob. Feed ear into sheller, crank handle, kernels drop into bucket. Fast and efficient. Essential for more than a few dozen ears. $50-$200.

# CHAPTER 3: THRESHING, WINNOWING, GRINDING, AND STORAGE
## Processing and Preserving Your Grain Harvest

**Threshing Wheat: Separating Grain From Straw**

After wheat is harvested and dried, grain must be separated from stalks (straw) and seed heads. This process is threshing.

**Traditional threshing methods:**

**Flail threshing:**

Lay dried wheat sheaves on clean, hard surface (threshing floor). Can be concrete, compacted earth, plywood, or heavy tarp.

Hold flail (wooden handle with shorter beater attached by leather strap or chain). Strike wheat repeatedly. Beater knocks grain loose from heads.

After 20-30 strikes per sheaf, grain is mostly free. Remove straw, keep grain and chaff (papery covering on grain).

**Rate:** One person can thresh 100-200 pounds of wheat per day with flail. Exhausting work but effective.

**Animal trampling:**

Historic method. Spread wheat on threshing floor. Lead horse, cow, or donkey in circles over wheat. Animal hooves crush seed heads and release grain.

After 20-30 minutes of trampling, grain is separated. Remove straw, collect grain and chaff.

**Advantage:** Less labor-intensive than flailing. **Disadvantage:** Requires calm, trained animal and clean hooves.

**Tarp beating:**

Modern adaptation. Place dried wheat sheaves in large tarp or bedsheet. Gather corners to create bag. Beat bag against fence, tree, or ground. Grain shakes loose and collects in tarp.

Less efficient than flailing but requires no tools. Good for small amounts.

**Makeshift barrel thresher:**

Place wheat heads in clean metal trash can. Use long stick or old weed-whacker (without string, just motor) to beat wheat heads inside can. Grain separates and falls to bottom. Pour off grain and chaff.

Surprisingly effective improvised method.

**Winnowing: Removing Chaff**

After threshing, you have mix of grain, chaff, straw fragments, and dirt. Winnowing separates grain from unwanted material using wind.

**Traditional winnowing:**

Wait for breezy day (10-15 mph wind ideal).

Stand on elevated surface (tailgate, deck, stepladder) with wind at your back.

Hold basket or bucket with threshed grain/chaff mix.

Pour slowly from basket to tarp or sheet on ground below. As grain falls, wind blows lightweight chaff away. Heavy grain falls straight down.

Repeat 2-3 times for clean grain.

**Fan winnowing:**

Modern shortcut. Set up box fan aiming across your work area.

Pour threshed grain/chaff slowly in front of fan. Chaff blows away, grain falls into bucket or onto tarp.

More controlled than natural wind. Works on calm days.

**Screen winnowing:**

Use series of screens (hardware cloth with different mesh sizes). Shake grain through screens. Chaff and large debris stay on top. Grain falls through. Small debris falls through finer screen. Grain remains on middle screen.

Effective for final cleaning after wind winnowing.

**Final result:** Clean wheat grain ready for storage or grinding.

**Grinding Grain Into Flour**

Whole grain stores for years. Flour goes rancid in months (oils in grain oxidize after grinding). Therefore: Store grain whole, grind as needed.

**Hand-crank grain mills:**

Most common homestead solution. Clamp to table, pour grain into hopper, crank handle. Adjustable burrs grind grain to desired coarseness (fine flour, cracked grain, or coarse meal).

**Stone burrs vs. steel burrs:** Stone produces finer, cooler flour (less oxidation from friction heat). Steel is faster and easier to maintain. Both work well.

**Grinding rate:** Quality hand mill grinds 1-2 cups flour per minute. That's 3-5 pounds per hour of cranking. Tiring but manageable for weekly baking needs.

**Brands:** Country Living Grain Mill (premium, $400-$500), Victorio (mid-range, $60-$80), Corona (basic, $40-$50).

**Electric grain mills:**

If electricity is available, electric mills grind 10-20 pounds per hour. Much faster than hand-grinding. But they're dependent on grid.

**Best strategy:** Own both. Use electric for convenience. Hand-crank as backup.

**Stone grinding:**

Traditional method. Two large circular stones (18-36 inches diameter). Grain pours into hole in center of top stone (runner stone). As top stone rotates, grain is crushed between stones and pushed outward. Flour emerges at edges.

Can be powered by hand-crank, animal walking in circle (sweep arm), water wheel, or wind.

Excellent for large-scale production or community mill. But expensive and requires infrastructure.

**Mortar and pestle:**

Ancient method. Place grain in sturdy bowl (mortar), crush with heavy club (pestle).

Works but extremely labor-intensive. Takes 30+ minutes of pounding to produce 1 cup flour. Only practical for small amounts or emergency.

**Grinding corn:**

Corn is harder than wheat. Requires more powerful mill or tighter burr setting.

**Cornmeal:** Coarsely ground corn. Used for cornbread, polenta, grits. Easiest to produce.

**Corn flour:** Finely ground corn. Requires tight burr setting and powerful mill. Used for tortillas (masa harina), baking.

**Nixtamalization:** Traditional Native American process. Soak corn in lime water (calcium hydroxide solution) overnight. Rinse. This softens corn, improves nutrition (releases niacin), and makes it easier to grind. The result is hominy. Grind wet hominy to produce masa dough for tortillas.

**Grinding oats:**

Oats have hull that must be removed before eating (oat groats). Hulled oats can be ground into flour or coarsely chopped for porridge.

**Steel-cut oats:** Groats chopped into 2-3 pieces with sharp blade or steel burr mill. Traditional breakfast oatmeal.

**Rolled oats:** Groats steamed and rolled flat. Requires special equipment (rolling mill). Most homesteaders buy rolled oats rather than producing.

**Oat flour:** Ground groats. Used in baking (though doesn't rise like wheat due to lack of gluten).

**Storing Grain Long-Term**

Properly stored, whole grain lasts 10-30 years. This is strategic food security.

**Enemy factors:**

**Moisture:** Above 14% moisture, grain molds. Keep grain dry.

**Oxygen:** Causes oxidation and allows aerobic pests. Limit oxygen exposure.

**Pests:** Weevils, grain moths, mice, rats. Exclude or kill pests.

**Temperature:** Heat accelerates degradation. Cool storage is better.

**Light:** UV degrades nutrients. Store in dark.

**Storage methods:**

**Food-grade buckets with gamma lids:**

Most common homestead method. 5-gallon buckets hold 30-35 pounds wheat. Use food-grade plastic (HDPE #2). Gamma lids seal tight but allow easy opening (unlike standard bucket lids).

Place grain in bucket. Add oxygen absorber packet (300-500cc). Seal immediately. Oxygen absorber removes oxygen, creating near-vacuum seal. Kills insect eggs and prevents spoilage.

Cost: $5-$8 per bucket, $8-$12 per gamma lid, $0.50 per oxygen absorber.

**Mylar bags in buckets:**

Extra protection. Line bucket with 5-gallon mylar bag. Fill with grain. Add oxygen absorber. Heat-seal mylar bag with iron. Then close bucket lid.

Mylar provides additional oxygen and moisture barrier. Redundant protection.

**Metal cans or drums:**

Galvanized steel trash cans with tight lids work well. 20-gallon can holds 100-120 pounds wheat. Place grain directly in can or use mylar bags inside can.

Metal protects from rodents better than plastic. But more expensive.

**Glass jars:**

For smaller amounts. Mason jars hold 3-4 pounds grain. Add oxygen absorber, seal. Convenient for frequently used grain (next month's supply).

**Location:**

Cool, dry, dark basement or root cellar is ideal. Avoid garages (temperature swings) or sheds (moisture).

**Rodent-proofing:** Place buckets/cans on shelves or pallets (not directly on floor). Reduces rodent access.

**Rotation:**

Mark containers with contents and date. Use oldest first. Though whole grain lasts decades, rotating ensures freshest supply.

**How Much Grain to Store**

For one year's complete grain self-sufficiency, per person:

**Wheat:** 400-500 pounds
**Corn:** 300-400 pounds
**Oats:** 100-150 pounds
**Total:** 800-1,050 pounds per person per year

Family of four: 3,200-4,200 pounds total grain per year.

If you're harvesting 40 bushels wheat per acre (2,400 pounds) and 100 bushels corn per acre (5,600 pounds), you'll have surplus for livestock feed, seed saving, and trade.

**Seed Saving**

Critical skill. Save best grain from each harvest for next year's planting.

**Selection criteria:**

Choose seed from:
- Healthiest plants
- Earliest maturing plants (ensures seed production even in shorter seasons)
- Largest seed heads/ears
- Disease-free plants

**Amount needed:**

**Wheat:** 90-120 pounds per acre (2-3 bushels). Save 5-10% of harvest for seed.

**Corn:** 8-10 pounds per acre. Save approximately 1% of harvest for seed.

Store seed grain separately from eating grain. Use same storage method (buckets with oxygen absorbers). Clearly label as SEED.

**Using Your Grain**

You've grown it, harvested it, threshed it, stored it. Now you eat it.

**Wheat uses:**

- Grind into flour for bread, tortillas, pasta, pastries, pancakes, biscuits
- Cook whole berries (wheat berries) as porridge or side dish (similar to rice)
- Crack coarsely for hot cereal (cracked wheat)
- Sprout for fresh greens in winter

**Corn uses:**

- Grind into cornmeal for cornbread, polenta, grits
- Nixtamalize for hominy and masa (tortillas)
- Pop dried kernels for popcorn (flint corn varieties)
- Cook whole as hominy
- Feed to livestock (chickens, pigs love corn)

**Oat uses:**

- Steel-cut or rolled for oatmeal/porridge
- Grind into flour for baking (mix with wheat flour)
- Feed to horses (excellent energy source)

**Barley/rye uses:**

- Bread (rye bread is traditional dark bread)
- Porridge
- Animal feed
- Brewing (malted barley for beer)

**The Grain Lord's Power**

When currency fails, grain becomes currency. When stores empty, grain is wealth.

Throughout history, those who controlled grain controlled everything. Empires rose and fell based on grain supply.

On a homestead or in a collapse community, the person with silos full of grain is the most valuable person present.

Your 2-3 acres of grain, properly managed, makes you more food-secure than 99% of population.

This is real food security. Not a pantry with two weeks of canned goods. Not a garden with vegetables.

**Grain. The foundation of civilization. The foundation of survival.**
