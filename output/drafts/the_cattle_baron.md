# THE CATTLE BARON
## Managing Cattle, Horses, and Pigs for Serious Food Production

**Archetype:** Homesteader
**Emotional Hook:** "One cow feeds your family for a year. A breeding herd feeds them forever. Here's how to build a livestock empire on your land."

---

# CHAPTER 1: WHY LARGE LIVESTOCK CHANGES EVERYTHING
## From Backyard Chickens to Real Agriculture

Most homesteaders start small. Chickens in the backyard. A few rabbits. Maybe some goats.

That's fine for learning. But it's not real food security.

Real food security comes from large livestock: Cattle. Horses. Pigs.

These animals represent the difference between gardening and agriculture. Between hobby farming and genuine food production. Between eating vegetables with occasional meat and having protein security for years.

**The Protein Problem**

Humans need protein. Not occasionally. Daily.

Your body requires approximately 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. For a 180-pound adult, that's about 65 grams daily. For a family of four, that's roughly 260 grams per day, or 95 kilograms per year.

Chickens are wonderful. But a chicken provides maybe 2-3 pounds of meat. To meet your family's annual protein needs from chickens alone, you'd need to raise and process approximately 200-300 chickens per year.

That's a chicken every 1-2 days. Forever.

One beef cow, by contrast, yields 400-600 pounds of meat. That's an entire year's protein for a family of four from one animal.

The math changes everything.

**Why Large Livestock Matters for Homestead Economics**

Large livestock changes your economic model from consumption to production.

**Feed conversion efficiency at scale:** Cattle, horses, and pigs can convert pasture, hay, and agricultural waste into meat with remarkable efficiency when managed properly. A single cow can convert one acre of pasture into 400+ pounds of beef per year.

**Capital livestock vs. consumable livestock:** Chickens are consumable. You raise them, process them, eat them, repeat. Cattle are capital. You maintain a breeding herd that produces calves annually. The herd is an asset that generates food continuously.

**Milk, meat, and labor:** A dairy cow provides both milk (5-7 gallons daily) and calves (meat). A horse provides labor (plowing, hauling, transportation). A breeding sow provides 20+ piglets per year (2,000+ pounds of pork annually from one animal).

**Trade value:** During economic collapse, a cow is worth more than gold to someone who needs food. Large livestock becomes currency in barter economies.

**The Traditional Homestead Model**

For thousands of years, successful farms operated on a mixed livestock model:

**Cattle:** Dairy for daily nutrition, beef for annual meat supply, breeding for herd growth and trade.

**Horses:** Primary labor source for plowing, hauling, and transportation. Essential for pre-industrial agriculture.

**Pigs:** Efficient converters of food waste and agricultural surplus into high-fat, high-protein meat. Require minimal land.

**Chickens/Small Livestock:** Eggs, supplemental meat, pest control in gardens.

This model persisted because it works. Modern industrial agriculture abandoned it for efficiency, but efficiency collapses when the systems fail.

**What You Need to Start**

Most people think large livestock is impossible without massive land. Not true.

**Minimum land requirements:**
- **2-5 acres:** Sufficient for 2-3 cattle with intensive rotational grazing and hay purchase
- **5-10 acres:** Comfortable for small cattle herd (5-8 animals) with some hay production
- **10-20 acres:** Full cattle operation with hay self-sufficiency
- **1 acre:** Sufficient for 2-3 pigs in managed system

**Infrastructure requirements:**
- Fencing (perimeter and rotational paddocks)
- Shelter (simple three-sided barn or loafing shed)
- Water source (pond, well, or reliable creek)
- Hay storage (barn or covered area)
- Handling facilities (basic corral and chute)

**Knowledge requirements:**
- Basic animal husbandry
- Pasture management
- Breeding and calving/farrowing
- Basic veterinary care
- Butchering or relationship with processor

**Capital requirements:**
- $3,000-$8,000 per breeding cow (regional variation)
- $500-$2,000 per breeding pig
- $1,000-$5,000 per working horse
- Fencing: $1-$3 per linear foot (depending on type)
- Basic shelter: $2,000-$10,000 (can be DIY much cheaper)

This is significant investment. But compare to ongoing food costs: A family of four spends $8,000-$12,000 annually on groceries. Your initial livestock investment pays for itself in 2-3 years, then produces food indefinitely.

**The Three Large Livestock Categories**

**Cattle (Beef and Dairy):**

Primary homestead livestock for most operations. Provides meat, milk, leather, labor (oxen), and breeding stock. Requires significant land (pasture) but converts grass into protein with minimal input once established.

**Advantages:** Large meat yield per animal, can live primarily on pasture, relatively low maintenance, high trade value, gentle temperament (most breeds), long productive life.

**Disadvantages:** Significant initial investment, requires fencing and handling facilities, long time to market weight (18-30 months), large financial loss if animal dies.

**Horses:**

Often overlooked in modern homesteading, but essential for grid-down agriculture. Provides transportation, agricultural labor (plowing, harrowing, hauling), and can be ridden for security, hunting, and travel when fuel is unavailable.

**Advantages:** Provides labor replacing tractors and vehicles, requires no fuel or electricity, reproduces labor force through breeding, long working life (15-25 years), high barter value, dual-purpose breeds can be ridden and worked.

**Disadvantages:** Requires training (both horse and handler), significant hay consumption, hoof care and veterinary needs, requires tack and equipment, dangerous if improperly handled.

**Pigs:**

The most efficient meat producer for small acreage. Converts food waste, garden surplus, and grain into pork with exceptional feed conversion ratios. Can be raised in small spaces with proper management.

**Advantages:** Fastest time to market (6-8 months), exceptional feed conversion, minimal space requirements, high reproduction rate (20+ piglets per sow per year), omnivorous (eat almost anything), natural tillers (prepare ground for planting).

**Disadvantages:** Require strong fencing (incredibly intelligent and strong), need consistent feed (less pasture-based than cattle), processing must be done properly to avoid trichinosis, can be aggressive (especially boars), smell can be significant if poorly managed.

**Making the Decision**

Your choice of large livestock depends on:

**Land availability:** 1-2 acres? Start with pigs. 5+ acres? Cattle become viable. 10+ acres? Consider horses for labor.

**Primary goal:** Maximum meat production? Pigs. Milk and meat? Dairy cattle. Labor for agriculture? Horses. Long-term capital and breeding herd? Beef cattle.

**Experience level:** Beginners should start with pigs (faster feedback loop, smaller investment per animal). Intermediate should consider cattle. Advanced should consider horses (training requirement is significant).

**Climate:** Pigs struggle in extreme heat without shade and water. Cattle tolerate cold exceptionally well but need wind breaks. Horses adapt to most climates but need shelter from extreme weather.

**Processing capability:** Can you butcher a 1,200-pound cow? Or is a 250-pound pig more manageable? Do you have access to mobile processing? Or do you need to trailer animals to processor?

Most successful large-livestock homesteads eventually include all three: cattle for baseline food security and capital, pigs for fast meat production and efficiency, horses for labor and transportation.

But you start with one. Learn it thoroughly. Then expand.

# CHAPTER 2: CATTLE OPERATIONS FROM FIRST COW TO BREEDING HERD
## Beef, Dairy, and Dual-Purpose Systems

**Choosing Your First Cow**

Your first cattle purchase determines your entire operation's trajectory. Choose wrong and you'll fight problems for years. Choose right and you'll build a thriving herd from solid foundation.

**Beef vs. Dairy vs. Dual-Purpose:**

**Beef cattle** are bred for meat production. Muscular build, efficient feed conversion to meat, calm temperament, excellent mothers. Beef cows produce enough milk for their calf but not significant excess for human use.

*Best beef breeds for homesteads:*
- **Angus:** Excellent meat quality, calm temperament, good mothers, adapt to most climates
- **Hereford:** Hardy, excellent foragers, calm, good for beginners
- **Devon:** Dual-purpose (beef and oxen), heritage breed, excellent foragers
- **Scottish Highland:** Extremely hardy, minimal care, excellent foragers, lean meat

**Dairy cattle** are bred for milk production. Lean build, high feed requirements, produce 5-7 gallons of milk daily. Require milking twice daily without fail. Dairy bull calves are processed for veal or raised for beef (slower growth than beef breeds).

*Best dairy breeds for homesteads:*
- **Jersey:** Smaller size (easier handling), rich milk (high butterfat), efficient feed conversion
- **Dexter:** Miniature breed, dual-purpose, 1-2 gallons daily, perfect for small homesteads
- **Milking Shorthorn:** Dual-purpose, good beef calves, moderate milk production

**Dual-purpose cattle** balance meat and milk. Heritage breeds developed before specialization. Produce 2-4 gallons milk daily and good beef calves. Best option for most homesteaders.

*Best dual-purpose breeds:*
- **Dexter:** Small (750-900 lbs), 1-2 gallons daily, excellent beef, perfect for beginners
- **Milking Shorthorn:** Full-size, 3-4 gallons daily, good beef calves
- **Devon:** Historic American breed, oxen capability, good beef, some milk

**Starting With One Cow vs. Breeding Pair**

Most experts recommend starting with a "family cow"—a trained, gentle, bred dairy or dual-purpose cow that provides milk and produces a calf annually.

This gives you:
- Immediate milk production (learning the skill)
- Annual calf for beef
- Experience with birthing, calf-rearing, and basic husbandry
- Lower initial cost (one animal vs. two)
- Gentler introduction (dairy cows are typically calmer and more handled than beef cows)

Alternative approach: Start with bred beef heifer (young female pregnant with first calf). Lower maintenance (no milking), less handling required, produces calf for beef, begins your breeding herd.

Avoid starting with: Bull (dangerous without experience), bottle calf (difficult to raise, imprints improperly), wild/unhandled cattle (dangerous), auction cattle (unknown health history).

**Fencing for Cattle**

Cattle respect fences. Unlike pigs (which test everything) or goats (which climb everything), cattle mostly stay where they belong if fencing is adequate.

**Perimeter fencing options:**

**High-tensile electric:** Most cost-effective for large areas. 5-7 strands of electrified wire. $0.50-$1 per linear foot. Requires charger (solar or AC). Psychological fence—cattle learn to respect it.

**Woven wire with barbed top:** More expensive ($2-$3 per foot) but permanent. Good for property boundaries. 4ft woven wire with single barbed strand on top.

**Board fencing:** Expensive ($8-$15 per foot) but attractive and permanent. Good for areas near home or road frontage.

**Minimum fence heights:** 4 feet for beef cattle, 5 feet for dairy (they're more athletic and flighty).

**Rotational grazing systems:**

Single large pasture is wasteful and damages soil. Rotational grazing divides pasture into paddocks, moving cattle every 1-7 days.

Benefits:
- Increases carrying capacity (more cattle per acre)
- Improves pasture health (even grazing, rest periods)
- Reduces parasites (cattle moved before parasite lifecycle completes)
- Improves soil fertility (concentrated manure, even distribution)

Implementation: Use temporary polywire electric fencing ($0.10-$0.20 per foot) to subdivide pasture. Move cattle to fresh paddock daily or every few days. Previously grazed paddocks rest 30-60 days before re-grazing.

**Water Systems**

Cattle drink 10-15 gallons per day in moderate weather, up to 30 gallons in heat.

**Water options:**

**Pond:** Best if available. Natural water source, minimal infrastructure. Fence cattle out except at access point to prevent overgrazing and pollution. Create stone-reinforced watering area.

**Well with trough:** Most common. Hand pump, solar pump, or electric pump fills stock tank. Automatic float valve maintains water level. Install heater in winter (prevents freezing).

**Creek/stream:** Excellent if reliable year-round. Fence to prevent overgrazing riparian area. Create reinforced crossing and watering point.

**Hauled water:** Last resort for small operations. 100-gallon stock tank filled via truck or tractor. Labor-intensive but workable for 1-3 cattle short-term.

**Shelter Requirements**

Cattle are remarkably hardy. They tolerate cold, heat, rain, and snow better than most livestock. Elaborate barns are unnecessary.

**Minimum shelter:** Three-sided loafing shed. 12x16 structure with roof and three walls. Open side faces away from prevailing wind. Provides wind break, shade, and dry area. Cost: $1,500-$3,000 DIY, $5,000-$10,000 built.

Alternative: Dense tree line or natural terrain features (hillside, bluff) providing wind break.

Cold weather: Cattle with full winter coats tolerate temperatures to -20°F if dry and out of wind. Calves need more protection—bedded area in loafing shed with deep straw.

Hot weather: Shade is critical. Trees, shade cloth, or roof. Cattle suffer heat stress above 80°F. Always provide shade and abundant water.

**Feed: Pasture, Hay, and Grain**

**Pasture:** Primary feed source spring through fall. One cow requires 1.5-2 acres of good pasture with rotational grazing, or 3-5 acres with continuous grazing.

**Hay:** Winter feed and pasture supplement during drought. One cow consumes 25-35 pounds of hay daily. That's 4-6 tons per year. Store hay dry and covered. Budget $100-$200 per ton (regional variation).

**Grain:** Optional for cattle on good pasture/hay. Necessary for dairy cows (supports milk production) and finishing beef cattle (increases marbling and growth rate). Common options: corn, oats, barley, commercial cattle feed. Feed 3-8 pounds daily depending on purpose.

**Mineral supplement:** Essential. Cattle need salt and mineral supplementation. Provide free-choice mineral block or loose minerals. $20-$40 per 50lb block, lasts 2-3 months per cow.

**Breeding and Calving**

**Breeding timing:** Cows cycle every 21 days when not pregnant. Bred cows carry calf for 283 days (approximately 9 months). Plan calving for spring (easier weather, growing grass available for milk production).

**Breeding options:**

**Keep a bull:** Requires additional fencing (strong enough to contain 2,000lb animal), separate housing, danger management. Bulls are dangerous—even gentle bulls can kill. Advantage: Natural breeding, self-sustaining herd.

**AI (Artificial Insemination):** Hire AI technician or learn to do it yourself. Requires detecting heat cycles (harder for beginners). Advantage: Access to superior genetics, no dangerous bull, lower cost.

**Lease/borrow bull:** Rent bull from neighbor for 2-3 months during breeding season. Pay fee ($200-$500) or give bull owner a calf. Advantage: No year-round bull maintenance.

**Calving management:**

Most calvings are uncomplicated. Cow goes off by herself, lays down, calf is born within 1-2 hours, cow cleans and nurses calf. Your job: Observe from distance, intervene only if problem.

**Normal calving timeline:**
- Stage 1 (2-6 hours): Cow restless, separates from herd, looks at flanks, may kick at belly
- Stage 2 (30min-2 hours): Active labor. Water breaks, calf appears, cow pushes
- Stage 3 (within 12 hours): Placenta expelled

**When to intervene:**
- Cow actively pushing for 2+ hours with no progress
- Only one foot visible (should be two front feet and nose)
- Calf backwards (back feet first—need to pull quickly)
- Cow down and can't stand after labor begins

**Pulling a calf:** Attach obstetric chains to calf's legs (above fetlocks). Pull with contractions—down and outward (toward cow's hocks). Pull steadily, don't jerk. Two strong people can usually assist delivery.

**Post-calving care:**
- Ensure calf nurses within 4 hours (gets colostrum—essential antibodies)
- Dip navel in iodine (prevents infection)
- Observe cow for retained placenta (should pass within 12 hours)
- Monitor calf for vigor—standing, nursing, alert

**Building a Breeding Herd**

Start with one or two cows. Learn the management. Then expand strategically.

**Year 1:** One bred cow. Produces calf. You learn calving, milking (if dairy/dual-purpose), basic care.

**Year 2:** Original cow bred again. Previous year's calf growing. Consider adding second bred cow.

**Year 3:** Two cows calving. You now have 2 calves + 2 yearlings + 2 cows = 6 animals. Herd growing.

**Year 4-5:** Keep best heifer calves as replacements. Sell or process bull calves and extra heifers. Establish stable herd size matching your land and feed capacity.

**Target herd size for homestead:** 3-5 breeding cows provides 3-5 calves annually. That's 1,200-2,000 pounds of beef per year plus dairy if you're milking. Sufficient for large family plus substantial surplus for trade.

# CHAPTER 3: HORSES FOR LABOR AND PIGS FOR EFFICIENCY
## The Complete Large Livestock System

**Why Horses Matter in Grid-Down Scenarios**

Modern homesteaders overlook horses. They're seen as luxury, recreational animals. This is profound mistake.

Horses are agricultural machines that reproduce themselves and run on grass.

**What horses replace:**
- Tractors (plowing, discing, harrowing)
- Trucks (hauling logs, firewood, supplies)
- ATVs (transportation, patrol, hunting)
- Generators (mechanical power for mill wheels, saws, pumps via horse-powered mechanisms)

When the power grid fails permanently, when fuel becomes unavailable or unaffordable, mechanized agriculture stops. Those with horses continue farming. Those without return to manual labor for every task.

**The numbers matter:**

One horse can plow 1 acre per day. By hand, that same acre takes 2-3 weeks of exhausting labor.

One horse can haul 1,000-2,000 pounds of cargo. By hand, you carry maybe 50 pounds.

One horse can transport you 30-40 miles per day. Walking, you cover maybe 15-20 miles.

Horses multiply your capability by an order of magnitude.

**Draft Horses vs. Riding Horses vs. Dual-Purpose**

**Draft horses** are built for pulling heavy loads. Breeds: Belgian, Percheron, Clydesdale, Suffolk Punch. Weigh 1,600-2,200 pounds. Strong, calm temperament, easy to train. Can pull 1.5-2x their body weight.

Advantages: Maximum pulling power, calm disposition, impressive size deters threats, excellent for serious agriculture.

Disadvantages: Expensive ($3,000-$8,000), consume large amounts of hay (30-40 lbs daily), require substantial harness equipment, too large for some riders.

**Riding horses** are lighter, faster, more maneuverable. Breeds vary widely: Quarter Horse, Morgan, Mustang, Arabian. Weigh 900-1,200 pounds.

Advantages: Versatile (riding, light farm work, hunting), lower feed costs, faster for transportation, easier to handle for most people.

Disadvantages: Less pulling power than draft breeds, less intimidating for security, can be flighty depending on breeding.

**Dual-purpose horses** balance riding and draft capabilities. Best breeds: Morgan, Norwegian Fjord, Haflinger, certain Quarter Horse lines.

These horses can be ridden for transportation, patrol, or hunting, AND used for farm work pulling carts, small plows, or logs. Weight 900-1,400 pounds.

**For most homesteaders:** Dual-purpose breed is optimal. Provides maximum versatility on limited budget.

**Basic Horse Care and Infrastructure**

**Fencing:** Horses require taller, stronger fencing than cattle. Minimum 5 feet tall. Best options: 4-board wooden fence, or woven wire with top board. Electric fence works but horses can injure themselves running through when spooked.

**Shelter:** Three-sided run-in shed minimum. 10x12 for one horse. Horses are hardy and can live outside year-round but need wind/rain break and shade.

**Feed:** Horse eats 2-3% of body weight daily in forage. A 1,000lb horse needs 20-30 pounds of hay daily. That's 3.5-5 tons per year. Pasture can replace hay in warm months. Grain supplementation needed for working horses (3-8 lbs daily depending on work load).

**Water:** 8-12 gallons daily, more in heat or during work.

**Hoof care:** Hooves must be trimmed every 6-8 weeks. Learn to do this yourself or pay farrier $40-$80 per visit. Working horses need shoes ($120-$200 per shoeing every 6-8 weeks).

**Tack and equipment:** Saddle, bridle, halter, lead ropes for riding. Harness, collar, traces, singletree, doubletree for draft work. Used equipment acceptable—check fit carefully. Budget $500-$2,000 for complete setup.

**Training Horses for Farm Work**

If you're new to horses, buy a trained draft horse or dual-purpose horse already broke to harness. Training a green horse to pull is advanced skill requiring experience.

**Working a trained horse:**

**Ground driving practice:** Before hitching to equipment, practice ground driving. Handler walks behind horse, controlling with long reins. Practice commands: Walk, whoa, gee (right turn), haw (left turn), back.

**Hitching to light equipment:** Start with lightweight items. Log chain with small log. Empty cart. Build horse's confidence.

**Progressing to implements:** Once horse is confident, hitch to actual farm equipment. Start with light loads, progress to heavier.

**Commands consistency:** Use same voice commands every time. Quiet, calm voice. Horses respond to tone and consistency, not volume.

**Common draft horse implements:**

**Plow:** Breaks new ground, turns under sod. Single-bottom walking plow for one horse, double-bottom for team. Adjust depth based on soil.

**Disc harrow:** Breaks up clods after plowing, prepares seedbed. Pull behind horse, discs rotate and chop soil.

**Cultivator:** Weed control between crop rows. Tines dig up weeds while avoiding crop plants.

**Cart/wagon:** Transportation and hauling. Two-wheeled cart for lighter loads and maneuverability. Four-wheeled wagon for heavier loads and stability.

**Log arch:** Lifts front of log off ground, allows horse to drag logs without getting bogged down.

**Ground-driven equipment:** Horse walks in circle, providing power to mechanical equipment via sweep arm. Historic method for sawmills, grain mills, water pumps.

**Using Horses for Security and Transportation**

Horses provide critical advantages in collapse scenarios:

**Patrol and security:** Mounted security can cover more ground, see farther, respond faster. Horse's height provides tactical advantage. Horse's senses (hearing, smell) detect threats humans miss.

**Hunting:** Access remote hunting areas, pack out game. Mounted hunters cover more territory. Horse carries harvested game.

**Communication and trade:** When vehicles don't run, horse provides reliable long-distance transportation. 30-40 miles per day sustainable. Carry 200+ pounds cargo in saddlebags and pack frame.

**Intimidation factor:** Person on horseback is psychologically intimidating to potential threats. Height, size, and unfamiliar animal create uncertainty in attackers.

**Psychological value:** Horses provide companionship, purpose, and connection to heritage during collapse. Powerful psychological stabilizer.

**Pigs: Maximum Meat From Minimum Space**

If cattle are capital livestock and horses are labor livestock, pigs are production livestock.

Pigs exist to convert feed into meat with exceptional efficiency, requiring minimal space and infrastructure.

**The pig advantage:**

**Feed conversion:** Pigs convert feed to meat at 3.5:1 ratio. That means 3.5 pounds of feed produces 1 pound of gain. Compare to cattle at 6:1 or 7:1. Pigs are nearly twice as efficient.

**Time to market:** Piglet to market weight (250 lbs) in 6 months. Cattle take 18-30 months. Pigs provide fast return.

**Reproduction:** Sows produce 2 litters per year, 10-14 piglets per litter. That's 20-28 piglets annually from one sow. Each piglet becomes 200+ pounds of pork. One sow produces 4,000-5,000 pounds of pork per year.

**Space requirement:** Pigs can be raised in small areas. 1/4 acre sufficient for 2-3 pigs. Compare to cattle requiring 2-5 acres.

**Waste conversion:** Pigs eat ANYTHING. Garden waste, spoiled food, wild foraging, grain. They're biological waste processors that produce meat.

**Choosing Pig Breeds**

**Heritage breeds:** These are the best choices for homesteads. They forage well, breed naturally, have strong mothering instincts, tolerate outdoor living.

**American Guinea Hog:** Smallest heritage breed (150-250 lbs). Excellent foragers, calm temperament, good mothers. Perfect for small homesteads. Lower meat yield but manageable processing.

**Berkshire:** Medium-large (600-700 lbs). Excellent meat quality (highly marbled), good foragers, calm. Traditional American farm pig.

**Tamworth:** Excellent foragers, bacon-type build (long body), very hardy, good mothers. Can be stubborn.

**Large Black:** Very docile, excellent mothers, great foragers. Rare breed, hard to source.

Avoid: Modern commercial breeds (Yorkshire, Landrace, Hampshire crosses) are bred for confinement. They don't forage well, have health issues outdoors, require careful breeding (often can't breed naturally).

**Pig Fencing: The Great Challenge**

Pigs are SMART. They test fences constantly. They're also STRONG. A 300-pound pig can uproot T-posts and push through woven wire.

**What works:**

**Electric fencing with proper training:** 2-3 strands of electric wire, bottom strand 6-8 inches off ground. But pigs must be TRAINED to respect electric fence (hairy coats insulate them). Training method: Confine pigs in small pen with electric fence they can't avoid. They get zapped, learn to respect it. Then release into larger area.

**Hog panels:** Rigid wire panels, 34 inches tall, welded construction. T-post every 8 feet. Most reliable option. $50-$70 per 16-foot panel. Expensive but lasts decades.

**Board fencing:** Wooden boards with small gaps. Strong and reliable. Expensive. Ensure boards are on INSIDE of posts (pigs push out, not in).

**Combination:** Hog panels as perimeter, electric strand inside as psychological deterrent.

**Never use:** Barbed wire (pigs ignore it), cattle panels (gaps too large, piglets escape), chicken wire (pigs destroy instantly).

**Feeding Pigs**

Pigs need high-calorie, high-protein feed to grow efficiently.

**Feed options:**

**Commercial pig feed:** Easiest option. Complete nutrition. Feed 3-4 pounds daily per 100 pounds of pig. Expensive ($15-$20 per 50lb bag). Fast growth.

**Garden/food waste + grain supplement:** Reduce feed costs by feeding garden surplus, kitchen scraps, spoiled produce. Supplement with whole corn, oats, or barley. Free foraging + supplemental grain = slower growth but much lower cost.

**Pasture/forage:** Pigs can get significant nutrition from pasture if moved regularly. "Salad bar" method: Moveable pen rotated daily through pasture. Pigs eat grass, weeds, grubs, roots. Supplement with grain for adequate growth.

**Pannage:** Historic method—pigs released into forest to eat acorns, nuts, roots. Essentially free feed. Supplement with grain for finishing.

**Rule:** Whatever feed method you choose, pigs need consistent calories and protein. Underfeeding extends time to market and reduces meat quality.

**Breeding Pigs**

**Keeping a boar:** Dangerous. Boars are aggressive, especially during breeding. Attack handlers. Require isolated, strong fencing. Only keep boar if running large operation (10+ sows).

**AI (Artificial Insemination):** Possible but less common than cattle AI. Limited access to technicians and semen.

**Borrow/lease boar:** Best option for homesteads. Neighbor with boar brings him for 2-3 weeks during breeding. Sow cycles every 21 days; boar ensures breeding. Pay fee or give owner a piglet.

**Farrowing (Pig Birth):**

Sows farrow (give birth) 114 days after breeding. They build nests using straw or hay.

**Farrowing setup:** Provide clean, dry area with deep bedding. Farrowing crate (optional) prevents sow from rolling on piglets. Heat lamp for piglets (they can't thermoregulate first week).

**Farrowing process:** Sow builds nest, becomes restless. Lies down, piglets born one at a time over 2-4 hours. 10-14 piglets typical. Sow eats placenta (normal).

**Post-farrowing care:**
- Ensure all piglets nurse within first hours (get colostrum)
- Warm piglets under heat lamp if cold
- Watch for crushed piglets (common cause of death)
- Clip needle teeth (sharp teeth can damage sow's teats)
- Provide extra feed to sow (milk production demands high calories)

**Weaning:** Piglets weaned at 6-8 weeks. Separate from sow, move to grower pen.

**The Complete Livestock System**

The most resilient homesteads integrate all three:

**Cattle:** Long-term protein security, capital livestock, dairy, breeding herd provides continuous food and trade goods.

**Horses:** Labor for agriculture, transportation, security. Replaces mechanization.

**Pigs:** Fast meat production, efficient waste conversion, high-calorie food source, rapid breeding provides trade income.

Combined, these three livestock types provide complete food security, labor independence, and economic foundation for true homestead autonomy.

Start with one. Master it. Expand to the others as land, capital, and experience allow.

The cattle baron, the horseman, and the pig farmer—these are the roles that built agriculture for millennia. They'll build it again when systems fail.
