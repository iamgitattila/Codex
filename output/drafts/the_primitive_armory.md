# THE PRIMITIVE ARMORY
## Crafting Bows, Arrows, Spears, Knives, and Throwing Weapons From Natural Materials

**Archetype:** Wilderness Survivor
**Emotional Hook:** "When your ammunition runs out, your survival depends on weapons you can make with your hands. Here's how our ancestors armed themselves for 100,000 years."

---

# CHAPTER 1: WHY PRIMITIVE WEAPONS MATTER
## The Arsenal That Never Runs Out

Modern survival assumes firearms. AR-15s, handguns, shotguns. Thousands of rounds stockpiled.

This is prudent. Firearms are effective defensive tools.

But firearms have a fatal weakness: They require ammunition.

**What happens when ammunition is gone?**

In long-term collapse, extended wilderness living, or isolated survival scenarios, ammunition runs out. You might have 5,000 rounds stockpiled. Seems like plenty. But if you're hunting for food, defending yourself from predators or threats, and surviving for years, that 5,000 rounds becomes 2,000, then 500, then zero.

Then what?

You return to the technology that armed humans for 100,000 years before gunpowder: Primitive weapons.

**Bows, spears, atlatls, slings, throwing sticks, clubs, knives made from stone and wood.** These weapons require no factory, no ammunition supply chain, no chemicals. They require only knowledge, natural materials, and skill.

**The Historical Reality**

Humans conquered every environment on Earth with primitive weapons. They killed mammoths, cave bears, and lions. They defended against rival tribes. They built empires.

Native Americans hunted buffalo, elk, and deer with bows and arrows made from wood, sinew, and stone. They fought wars with these weapons. They survived and thrived for thousands of years without firearms.

Australian Aboriginals lived in one of the harshest environments on Earth for 65,000+ years using spears, throwing sticks (boomerangs), and clubs.

African hunter-gatherers still hunt successfully with poison arrows and spears.

**These weapons work.** They're not obsolete. They're renewable.

**The Capabilities and Limitations**

Primitive weapons are not firearms. Understand their realistic capabilities:

**Effective range:**

**Bow:** 20-40 yards for hunting (accurate shot placement). Maximum range 100-150 yards (war/harassment, low accuracy).

**Spear (thrust):** Close range, 0-10 feet. Requires engaging target directly.

**Spear (thrown):** 10-30 feet effective range. Low accuracy beyond 20 feet without significant practice.

**Atlatl (spear thrower):** 30-60 yards effective range. Significantly extends throwing spear range.

**Sling:** 50-100 yards effective range. Requires extensive practice for accuracy. Used historically for warfare and hunting.

**Stopping power:**

**Well-made bow with stone or metal broadhead arrow:** Can kill deer, elk, bear, and humans. Penetrates 4-8 inches into tissue. Causes massive hemorrhage from cut arteries. Death typically occurs in 30 seconds to several minutes.

**Thrusting spear:** Massive penetration (12+ inches possible). Historically used for bear, boar, and large game. Highly lethal at close range.

**Stone knife:** Lethal in close combat. 3-4 inch blade can sever major arteries and cause rapid incapacitation.

**Limitations:**

**Lethality is slower than firearms.** Arrow or spear wound causes death through hemorrhage, not hydrostatic shock. Animals and humans can fight for 30-120 seconds after lethal hit. This is dangerous. Wounded game can escape. Wounded attacker can still harm you.

**Accuracy requires practice.** Firearms are relatively easy to shoot accurately. Bows and thrown weapons require hundreds of hours of practice to achieve consistent accuracy.

**Close range required.** Most primitive weapons require getting closer to target than firearms. This increases danger and difficulty.

**Despite these limitations, primitive weapons are effective tools in skilled hands.** And skill can be developed.

**The Materials**

Every primitive weapon requires natural materials. Learning to identify and process these materials is half the skill.

**Wood:**

Different weapons require different wood characteristics.

**Bow wood:** Must be strong, elastic, and flexible. Best woods:
- **Osage orange:** Arguably best bow wood in North America. Dense, elastic, strong. Bright orange/yellow color.
- **Yew:** Traditional English longbow wood. Excellent compression and tension properties.
- **Hickory:** Very strong, good compression strength. Common in eastern US.
- **Ash:** Light, strong, good snap. Excellent for simple bows.
- **Maple:** Strong and available. Works well for beginner bows.
- **Elm:** Good tension wood. Often used in backed bows.

**Avoid:** Cottonwood, willow, pine (too soft and weak for bows).

**Spear/arrow shaft wood:** Must be straight-grained, lightweight, and stiff.
- **Ash:** Excellent for arrows and spear shafts.
- **Cedar:** Lightweight, straight, resistant to warping.
- **Birch:** Straight-grained, good for arrows.
- **Dogwood, viburnum, arrow-wood:** Traditional arrow woods (named for this purpose).
- **Bamboo:** Not native to North America but widely available. Excellent arrow and spear material.

**Club wood:** Dense, heavy, shock-resistant.
- **Hickory:** Extremely strong.
- **Oak:** Dense and heavy.
- **Ash:** Strong with good shock resistance.

**Stone:**

**Knapping stone** (can be fractured with controlled flakes to create sharp edges):
- **Flint/chert:** Most common knapping stone. Available across much of North America and Europe.
- **Obsidian:** Volcanic glass. Sharpest edge achievable (sharper than surgical steel). Found in volcanic regions.
- **Jasper:** Harder than flint, knaps well.
- **Basalt:** Volcanic rock, knaps okay but less fine than flint.
- **Quartzite:** Can be knapped but more difficult.

**How to identify knappable stone:** Strike two rocks together. If one produces sharp flakes (not crumbles), it can be knapped. Look for conchoidal fracture pattern (smooth, curved break like broken glass).

**Other Materials:**

**Sinew:** Animal tendon. Strongest natural cordage. Used for bow backing, arrow binding, bowstring reinforcement.
- Source: Leg tendons of deer, elk, cattle, or any large mammal.
- Process: Remove tendon, dry, pound to separate fibers, soak briefly before use.

**Hide glue:** Made from boiled animal hide, sinew, or hooves. Waterproof adhesive for arrows and bow backing.

**Feathers:** Arrow fletching. Stabilizes arrow in flight.
- Best: Turkey, goose, hawk, owl (large wing feathers).
- Need 3 feathers per arrow (split and shaped).

**Natural cordage:** Bowstrings and binding.
- **Plant fibers:** Dogbane, milkweed, nettle, yucca, hemp. Process fibers, twist into cord.
- **Rawhide:** Untanned leather strips. Strong but stretchy.
- **Sinew:** Best bowstring material for backed bows.
- **Linen or dacron:** Not primitive, but if available, superior bowstring material.

**Bone/antler:** Tool material for pressure flaking stone, arrowheads, spear points.

**The Primitive Weapon Arsenal**

The complete primitive weapons system includes:

1. **Bow and arrows:** Primary hunting weapon. Silent, accurate at moderate range, reusable ammunition.

2. **Spears:** Backup hunting weapon, close-range defense, fishing. Multiple spears carried.

3. **Knife:** Essential tool and weapon. Used for butchering, craft work, and defense.

4. **Atlatl and darts:** Extends spear range dramatically. Excellent for hunting and warfare.

5. **Throwing sticks/clubs:** Rapid deployment weapon for small game and defense.

6. **Sling:** Long-range harassment weapon. Small, packable, uses stone ammunition available anywhere.

A skilled primitive weapons practitioner carries:
- Bow with 12-24 arrows
- 2-3 spears
- Knife
- Atlatl with 3-6 darts (optional, if hunting large game)
- Sling
- Throwing stick or club

**This arsenal provides capability for hunting anything from rabbits to elk, defending against predators and humans, and surviving indefinitely in wilderness.**

# CHAPTER 2: MAKING BOWS AND ARROWS
## From Tree to Weapon

**Selecting and Harvesting Bow Wood**

**When to cut:**

Winter is best. Sap is down, wood is drier, less likely to check (crack) during drying. Avoid spring (high sap content causes warping and checking).

**Choosing the tree:**

Look for straight-grained wood without knots, twists, or branches for the section you'll use. Bow must be cut from single piece of wood (self bow), ideally 6 feet long minimum.

**For a 6-foot bow, you need 6-foot section of trunk or large branch with no branches, knots, or curves.**

This is harder to find than you think. Many trees must be examined. The straightest, clearest-grained wood makes the best bows.

**Cutting:**

Cut sapling or branch 1-2 inches wider than final bow dimensions (allows for shaping). If using trunk wood, split log to isolate straight-grained section following growth rings.

**Sealing ends:**

Immediately after cutting, seal ends with wax, glue, or paint. This prevents end-grain checking (cracks that ruin wood). Critical step.

**Drying:**

Store bow stave (roughed-out bow blank) in dry, shaded area for 3-6 months. Do not rush. Wet wood makes weak bow that breaks.

Alternatively, if you need bow immediately, select dead standing wood (already dry). Must be sound (not rotted or damaged by insects). Dead standing wood can be worked immediately.

**Layout and Design**

**Bow length:** For average adult (5'8"-6' tall), bow should be 65-68 inches long. Taller person needs longer bow. Shorter person can use shorter bow. General rule: Bow should be roughly your height plus 4-6 inches.

**Bow width:** 1.5 inches wide at handle, tapering to 0.5 inches at tips.

**Handle location:** Slightly above center (3-4 inches above midpoint). This creates longer lower limb, shorter upper limb. Balances bow when held horizontally.

**Bow profile:** Back of bow (side facing away from archer) should follow single growth ring (prevents breaking). Belly of bow (side facing archer) is carved to create proper thickness.

**Shaping the Bow**

**Remove bark:** Use knife to peel off bark. Expose sapwood or heartwood (depending on bow design).

**Determine back:** For best bows, back should be single continuous growth ring (visible as line in wood). Carefully remove wood down to one complete ring. Do not cut through this ring—any violation creates weak point where bow breaks.

**Shape profile:** Looking from back of bow, outline should taper from wide handle to narrow tips. Mark outline with charcoal or pencil. Remove wood outside outline using hatchet, knife, or drawknife.

**Shape thickness:** This is critical step. Belly (inside curve) must be thicker at handle, thinner toward tips. Too thick and bow won't bend. Too thin and bow breaks.

**Tillering method:**

1. Carve bow to approximately correct dimensions (thicker than final, to be safe).

2. String bow with temporary string (don't pull to full draw yet).

3. Hold bow horizontally, push down on handle while pulling up on string. Observe how limbs bend.

4. Both limbs should bend evenly in smooth arc. Stiff spots (parts that don't bend) must be carved thinner. Weak spots (parts that bend too much) are left alone (everywhere else is carved to match).

5. Gradually remove wood from stiff areas. Re-check bend frequently. This is slow process requiring patience.

6. Once bow bends evenly, begin pulling to progressively longer draw lengths (12 inches, 16 inches, 20 inches, 24 inches, 28 inches). At each stage, check for even bend and adjust.

7. When bow pulls smoothly to your draw length (typically 26-28 inches) without stiff or weak spots, tillering is complete.

**Final draw weight:** Depends on how much wood you removed. Hunting bow should be 40-60 pounds draw weight (measured at your draw length). Lighter bows (30-40 lbs) work for small game and practice. Heavier bows (60-80 lbs) for large game and warfare.

**Finishing:**

Sand smooth (prevents splinters). Seal with oil, beeswax, or animal fat (protects from moisture). Some bowyers heat-treat belly by carefully heating over coals (increases compression strength).

**Making Bowstring**

Bowstring must be strong, non-stretchy, and correct length.

**Materials:**

**Linen thread:** Historical material. Strong, minimal stretch. Wax thoroughly before use.

**Dacron (B-50):** Modern synthetic. Ideal material if available. Low stretch, strong, durable.

**Sinew:** Traditional material. Very strong but requires glue-backing on bow to prevent over-stressing wood (sinew stretches more than modern materials).

**Plant fiber cordage:** Dogbane or milkweed fiber twisted into heavy cord. Requires thick cord (minimal stretch needed). Least durable option but fully primitive.

**Construction:**

**Flemish twist:** Traditional method. Multiple strands twisted together into two bundles, then bundles twisted around each other. Forms loops at both ends for attaching to bow.

**Endless loop:** Continuous loop wrapped around two posts at correct distance, then serving (binding) is wrapped at each end to form loops.

**Length:** String should be 3-4 inches shorter than bow (measured from nock to nock when bow is unstrung). This creates proper brace height (distance from handle to string) of 6-7 inches.

**Making Arrows**

Arrows are harder to make than most people realize. Poor arrows fly crooked, miss targets, and break easily.

**Selecting shaft wood:**

Cut straight shoots or saplings, 30-32 inches long, 3/8 inch diameter. Perfectly straight is critical. Any curve causes arrow to fly crooked.

**Best woods:** Ash, cedar, birch, dogwood, bamboo (if available).

**Drying:** Bundle shafts, hang in dry area for 2-3 months. Or use dead, dry branches if available.

**Straightening:**

Wooden arrows are rarely perfectly straight naturally. Heat-straightening fixes this.

**Method:** Heat crooked section over coals (not in flame—you'll burn it). When hot enough to barely touch, bend opposite the curve. Hold until cool. Check straightness by rolling on flat surface—crooked arrow wobbles. Repeat until straight.

**Nock (rear notch):**

Cut small notch in rear of arrow for bowstring. Notch should be perpendicular to grain (prevents splitting). Width should fit bowstring snugly. Depth about 1/4 inch.

Wrap thread or thin sinew around shaft behind nock (prevents splitting when shot).

**Fletching (feathers):**

Arrows need 3 or 4 feathers to stabilize flight.

**Preparing feathers:** Large wing feathers from turkey, goose, hawk, or similar bird. Split feather down central quill (rachis), creating two matched halves. Cut to 4-5 inches long, 1/2 inch tall.

**Attachment:** Glue feather to rear of shaft using hide glue, pine pitch, or modern adhesive. Bind front and rear of each feather with thin thread or sinew. Space feathers evenly (120 degrees apart for 3 feathers, 90 degrees for 4 feathers).

**One feather (cock feather) should be perpendicular to nock.** This orients arrow correctly on bow (cock feather points away from bow when nocked).

**Arrowhead (point):**

**Stone points:** Knapped from flint, obsidian, or other stone. Shapes: Triangular, leaf-shaped, or notched. Size: 1.5-3 inches long, 0.75-1.5 inches wide.

Attach by cutting slot in front of arrow shaft, inserting point, wrapping tightly with sinew, and sealing with hide glue or pitch.

**Bone points:** Carved from leg bones of deer, elk, or cattle. Sharpened to point, often barbed. Attached same as stone.

**Fire-hardened points:** No stone or bone available? Cut tapered point on arrow shaft itself, harden in fire (carefully char surface without burning). Weakest option but functional for small game.

**Metal points:** If available (salvaged nails, metal scraps), metal makes excellent arrowheads. Sharpen, flatten into blade shape, attach as stone points.

**Finished arrow weight:** Should be 400-600 grains (roughly 1 ounce). Heavier arrows hit harder but drop faster. Lighter arrows fly flatter but penetrate less.

# CHAPTER 3: SPEARS, ATLATLS, AND STONE KNIVES
## The Complete Primitive Weapons Kit

**Spear Construction**

Spear is oldest weapon in human arsenal. Simple, effective, versatile.

**Design:**

**Length:** 6-8 feet for thrusting spear (long enough to keep dangerous animal at distance). 5-6 feet for throwing spear (shorter is more maneuverable).

**Shaft:** Straight hardwood pole, 1-1.25 inches diameter. Must be strong—spear experiences significant stress on impact.

**Best woods:** Ash, hickory, oak, bamboo.

**Point:**

**Stone:** Large spear points are easier to make than small arrowheads (less precise flaking required). Triangular or leaf-shaped, 3-5 inches long, 1.5-2 inches wide.

Attach by splitting shaft end, inserting point, binding with sinew, sealing with pitch or hide glue.

**Fire-hardened:** Cut tapered point on shaft, char in coals. Produces hard, sharp point. Must be re-sharpened after heavy use.

**Bone:** Sharpened bone (leg bones of deer/elk) lashed to shaft. Very sharp, effective for fishing and hunting.

**Metal:** If available, metal is superior. Sharpened rebar, filed metal scrap, or salvaged knife blades lashed to shaft.

**Forked spear (fishing):** Instead of point, cut shaft end into 3-4 prongs. Sharpen each prong, spread apart slightly. Creates multi-pointed fishing spear that pins fish against river bottom.

**Cross-bar (boar spear):**

For dangerous game (boar, bear), add cross-bar 12 inches behind point. Prevents animal from charging up shaft after being speared.

Cross-bar is short stick (6-8 inches) lashed perpendicular to shaft. Seems simple, but it saves your life when 300-pound boar is trying to reach you after being speared.

**Using Spears**

**Thrusting:** Hold spear in both hands, rear hand near butt, front hand 2-3 feet forward. Thrust forward explosively, driving point into target. Immediately withdraw and prepare for second thrust.

For large dangerous game: Brace spear butt against ground or hold firmly. Allow charging animal to impale itself. Cross-bar stops animal from reaching you.

**Throwing:** Hold spear at balance point (typically 1/3 from rear). Arm cocked back, then throw overhand (like baseball pitch) or sidearm. Effective range: 15-30 feet.

Throwing spear is backup weapon—once thrown, you're unarmed until you retrieve it. Carry multiple spears if throwing.

**The Atlatl: Ancient Weapon Multiplier**

Atlatl (also called spear-thrower) is mechanical advantage device that extends throwing range and power dramatically.

**Design:**

18-24 inch wooden handle with hook or socket at rear end. Hook engages rear of dart (specialized throwing spear). Thrower holds atlatl, which holds dart. When thrown, atlatl acts as arm extension, increasing leverage and velocity.

**Effect:** Atlatl increases throwing range by 2-3x compared to hand-thrown spear. Increases velocity significantly (darts can penetrate deeply into large game).

**Construction:**

Cut straight, strong stick 18-24 inches long, 1 inch thick. Comfortable grip at front end. Rear end has hook (carved from same wood) or socket (drilled hole with peg inserted).

Some designs include finger loops carved into handle for secure grip. Some include stone or bone weight near center (increases momentum during throw).

Atlatl is simple tool. Effective versions can be made in 2-3 hours from straight branch.

**Darts:**

Atlatl darts are shorter, lighter, and thinner than hand-thrown spears. Length: 4-6 feet. Diameter: 1/2 inch. Weight: 1-2 ounces.

Rear end has small cup or notch that engages atlatl hook.

Front end has stone, bone, or fire-hardened point (similar to spear points but lighter).

**Fletching:** Some darts have feather fletching (like oversized arrows) for stability. Others have no fletching.

**Using Atlatl:**

Hold atlatl in throwing hand, dart resting along atlatl with dart butt engaged in hook. Throwing motion is whip-like—start with arm cocked back, then accelerate forward explosively, releasing at peak speed.

Atlatl remains in hand. Dart launches forward.

**Effective range:** 30-60 yards for large game. Up to 100 yards for harassment/warfare (accuracy declines at distance).

**Historical use:** Atlatl was primary hunting weapon in Americas for thousands of years before bow and arrow. Aztecs used atlatl in warfare against Spanish conquistadors. Australian Aboriginals still use spear-throwers (woomera) for hunting.

**Stone Knife Manufacture (Flintknapping)**

Stone knife is essential tool and weapon. Learning to create sharp stone blades from raw rock is fundamental primitive skill.

**Knapping Basics:**

Flintknapping is controlled fracturing of stone to create sharp edges and desired shapes.

**Tools needed:**

**Hammerstone:** River-rounded rock (baseball-sized) for percussion flaking (striking large flakes off core).

**Billet:** Antler tine or hardwood stick for finer percussion flaking.

**Pressure flaker:** Antler tine or strong bone tool with pointed tip. Used to press small flakes off edge (fine shaping).

**Leather pad:** Protects leg while holding stone during knapping.

**Safety glasses:** Flakes are sharp and occasionally fly toward face. Eye protection essential.

**Creating a Knife Blank:**

**Select knappable stone:** Fist-sized or larger piece of flint, chert, obsidian, or jasper.

**Create striking platform:** Use hammerstone to strike edge of core, creating flat platform for next blow.

**Drive off blade blank:** Strike downward at angle onto platform. Large flake detaches. This flake is knife blank.

**Goal:** Create flake roughly 3-4 inches long, 1-2 inches wide, 1/4 inch thick. May require multiple attempts—knapping is skill requiring practice.

**Shaping the Knife:**

Hold blade blank in leather-protected hand. Use pressure flaker (antler tine) to press small flakes off edge. Work around edge, creating sharp cutting surface.

**Blade shapes:**

**Leaf shape:** Symmetrical, pointed at both ends. Efficient cutting shape.

**Drop point:** One edge straight, one edge curved to central point. Strong tip.

**Tanto:** Straight edge with angled tip. Strong for piercing.

Shape depends on blank form and intended use. Don't force specific shape—work with stone's natural form.

**Hafting (Adding Handle):**

Stone blade alone is functional but handle greatly improves control and safety.

**Split-stick handle:** Cut slot in end of straight stick (6-8 inches long). Insert blade into slot. Bind tightly with sinew or cordage. Seal with pitch or hide glue.

**Wrapped handle:** Wrap leather, cord, or bark around rear (dull) portion of blade. Provides grip without separate handle piece.

**Edge Maintenance:**

Stone blades dull with use. Re-sharpen by pressure flaking edge to remove dull sections and expose fresh sharp edge.

Obsidian stays sharper longer than flint. Flint is tougher (less prone to breaking).

**Other Stone Tools:**

**Axe head:** Large, heavy stone shaped to broad cutting edge. Hafted perpendicular to handle. Used for woodcutting, not combat (too slow).

**Scraper:** Rounded stone with steep edge. Used for scraping hides, working wood. Essential for leather processing.

**Drill:** Long, narrow stone point. Spun between palms or with bow drill to create holes in wood, bone, leather, or shell.

**Slings and Throwing Sticks**

**Sling:**

Ancient ranged weapon. Simple (just cord and pouch), deadly in skilled hands (remember David and Goliath).

**Construction:** Two cords (each 18-24 inches) attached to small pouch (leather, woven plant fiber, or cloth). One cord has finger loop at end. Other cord has knot or tag.

**Ammunition:** Smooth stones (egg-sized), lead bullets (if metal available), or ceramic/fired clay balls.

**Use:** Place projectile in pouch. Hold finger loop in throwing hand, pinch tag between fingers. Swing sling in circles overhead or sidearm, building speed. Release tag at proper moment—projectile launches at high velocity.

**Effective range:** 50-100 yards with practice. Sufficient force to kill small game or injure humans at close range.

**Problem:** Requires extensive practice. Accuracy is very difficult. But sling is tiny, weighs nothing, and ammunition is available anywhere. Worth carrying as backup weapon.

**Throwing Stick (Rabbit Stick):**

Curved or straight stick (18-24 inches) thrown at small game. Curved version is non-returning boomerang.

**Use:** Throw sidearm at rabbits, birds, or other small game. Doesn't penetrate like arrow, but impact breaks bones and stuns game.

**Effective range:** 10-30 feet.

**Advantage:** Silent, reusable, easy to make, can carry multiple sticks.

**The Completed Arsenal**

With knowledge from these three chapters, you can construct complete primitive weapons system:

- Self bow from hardwood (ash, hickory, osage orange)
- 12-24 arrows with stone points and feather fletching
- 2-3 spears (thrusting and throwing designs)
- Atlatl with 4-6 darts
- Stone knife with handle
- Sling with ammunition
- Throwing sticks for small game

**This arsenal is sufficient for hunting all North American game, defending against predators and threats, and surviving indefinitely in wilderness without resupply.**

Every component can be made from natural materials using stone-age technology. Nothing requires factories, supply chains, or industrial processes.

This is the armory that never runs out.
